How well does Aegex detect IoT attacks?

All numbers on this page come from a realistic benchmark: real attack traffic hidden inside the real normal traffic of a busy device, test data only, alarm lines set on validation data. On Edge-IIoTset and Kitsune the VAE catches almost every attack window while flagging about 1–8 % of normal windows. The GAT then names the attacking host with far fewer false alarms than the window alerts alone. On X-IIoTID, a 32-device industrial network added this week, the VAE catches 90.7 % of mixed-in attacks at 1.1 % false alarms once its alarm lines are set per device. Two more networks followed: on Gotham, the only one with Mirai, DDoS and scanning together, the VAE catches 96.5 % at 0.19 % false alarms, and on ACI-IoT-2023 (70 devices, run on Kaggle) 80.3 % at 1.7 %. On NF-ToN-IoT-v3, a busy 55-device network whose attacks are naturally mixed with normal traffic, it catches 94.2 % at 3.5 % false alarms, including 93–99 % of ransomware, which gives ransomware its second source. IoT-23 remains weak. The GAT (gat_v5, trained without port/service inputs so it cannot rely on giveaways such as "any Telnet link") finds the attacking hosts on Gotham, IoT-23 and ACI-IoT-2023 at 93.5–99.9 % with 0.2–1.4 % false alarms, far above the best one-input rule, but it does not recognise attack types or networks it was never trained on.

What was built

The detection pipeline so far

Capturepcap or flow logs from the network
Flowspackets grouped into two-way connections (5-tuple, 120 s records)
50 featuressizes, rates, timing, TCP flags, service, device behaviour
LSTM autoencoderreads 32 flows of one device at a time
VAEconfidence, closest known attack, zero-day flag
Temporal GATevery 60 s: which hosts are attacker and target
Analysis · RAG reportnext: attack analysis, Blue Team report

The LSTM and VAE learn only from normal traffic, so they can react to attacks they were never shown. The GAT is trained with attack labels; it turns window alerts into a short list of hosts and connections.

Reading the numbers

Terms used on this page

Window
32 consecutive connections of one device. The models judge windows, not single packets.
Detection rate
Of the attack windows, the share the system flagged. Higher is better.
False-alarm rate
Of the normal windows, the share the system wrongly flagged. Lower is better; the target is about 1 %.
ROC-AUC
How well the scores separate attacks from normal traffic, ignoring where the alarm line is drawn. 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing.
Calibration
Setting the alarm line from a sample of the target network's own normal traffic before monitoring it.
Per-device alarm line
After calibration, each device with enough normal traffic also gets its own line from that traffic, never lower than the network's. Devices that are noisy by nature stop flooding the alerts.
Noise floor
Share of windows flagged when plain normal traffic is inserted exactly the way attacks are. It shows how much the insertion itself triggers; a detector is only credible well above it.
One-line rule
The single best window statistic with a 1 % false-alarm line, chosen on validation data. A model that cannot beat it adds nothing.

What we foundEvidence
The dataset's own test split is too easyVAE 98.7 % caught, but a one-line timing rule gets 95.7 %. X-IIoTID rounds timestamps to the second, so 99 % of DDoS and 91 % of scan connections share a second with the previous one; only 36 % of normal ones do. Mixed into normal traffic, the rule falls to 74.5 %.
The LSTM alone catches almost nothing0–5 % caught. Floods and scans are repetitive and easy to rebuild, and the network's varied normal traffic sets the alarm line high. The VAE, which reads the LSTM's summaries, is what detects. Plain explanation: X-IIoTID LSTM Blind Spot.
False alarms came from two chatty devices, not from time188 of 190 false alarms were on two devices that send a lot of discovery and multicast traffic to many peers; every other device stayed at 0–1.3 %. Per-device alarm lines fixed it without losing a single detection.
One shared model for all four networks is worseTraining one pair on all four datasets cut IoT-23 detection from 83.6 % to 60.3 % and did not improve X-IIoTID (90.3 % vs 90.7 %), so the networks keep separate models.

Per-device alarm lines also work on live captures (score_traffic.py --calibrate normal.pcap --recalibrate-threshold --per-device). On Kitsune's 21-device Mirai capture they removed the remaining false alarms (0.19 % → 0 %) with the same detection. They are not a universal default: on Edge-IIoTset recalibration costs detection (99.9 % → 85.9 %), and IoT-23 has too little normal traffic to calibrate on.

Stage 3 · temporal GAT

Which host is attacking, every minute

Same benchmark, judged per host per 60 seconds. Model gat_v5: each network feeds the GAT through its own LSTM-AE and VAE, and the GAT is not given any port or service inputs (Telnet, HTTP, "registered port" and so on). An earlier version leaned on them: on Gotham, "any Telnet link" alone flagged 85 % of attackers at 0 % false alarms, because only Mirai uses Telnet there. Without them the GAT has to use behaviour: connection rates, peers, replies, TCP flags and volumes.

Two comparisons: the window alerts alone (a host counts as flagged if the LSTM or VAE raised any alert for it that minute) and the best one-input rule (the single GAT input that separates attackers best, chosen and thresholded on validation data only). The GAT only counts for what it adds above that rule.

Weak spots: on ACI-IoT-2023 the GAT finds only 37.5 % of DDoS host-minutes (32 in total) and ranks the true attacker first 41.7 % of the time; there, "many connections in 10 seconds" alone already gets 81.5 %. Hosts that only received extra normal traffic are flagged 0–2.3 % of the time on every training network, so the GAT is not keying on how attacks were inserted.

Networks it never trained on

On Kitsune and Edge-IIoTset, which were left out of training on purpose, the GAT flags about 40 % of normal hosts (Kitsune 42.6 %, Edge 39.2 % false alarms). It learns each network's own behaviour and does not carry over, so a deployment needs the GAT trained or fine-tuned on a recording of the network it protects.

Reality check

Why the datasets' own test splits are not quoted

On the datasets' own test splits both models score close to 100 %. We checked what those test sets actually ask the models to do.

On Edge-IIoTset one number ("average packets per connection" in the window) separates the attacks almost perfectly, because its attack recordings contain no normal traffic. That is why the main result above uses the benchmark instead: there the same one-line rule falls to 32 %.

Key finding

A new network needs calibration, and the VAE keeps detection after it

How we got here

Problems found and fixed

Several early results were too good to be true. Each one was traced to a cause and fixed in the code, and the fix is now part of every run.

  1. IoT-23 labelled 8.25 M Mirai scans as normal

    The same behaviour is labelled malicious in another IoT-23 capture. Relabelled by behaviour, keeping the original label for traceability. The in-capture baseline dropped from a memorised 1.000 to an honest 0.926.

  2. Scaling made attacks look 100 % detectable

    Very regular sensor traffic made tiny deviations look extreme. Edge-IIoTset DDoS detection fell from 100 % to 38 % after a small change to the training data. A minimum scale and soft limits fixed it: 100 % and stable.

  3. Single connections could not show scanning or floods

    Added device-behaviour features: connections per 10 s, new hosts and ports per minute, share of replies. IoT-23 DDoS detection went from 0.6 % to 95 %.

  4. Infected devices were teaching the model what "normal" is

    90 % of IoT-23's normal-labelled traffic came from the bots themselves. Those devices are now excluded from the normal reference. IoT-23 ROC-AUC went from 0.21 to 0.9998.

  5. One large capture defined "normal" for a whole dataset

    Kitsune's headline 1.7 % false alarms hid 22 to 44 % on its Mirai network. Training now samples every capture equally and every report lists false alarms per capture.

  6. Dataset test splits were trivially easy

    A one-line rule matched the models. Built the realistic benchmark: real attacks hidden in real normal traffic, test data only. This is now the only source of quoted numbers.

  7. Averaging hid small attacks inside a window

    A fast port scan on a busy sensor was caught 5 % of the time. Windows are now scored by their 8 most unusual connections, and the VAE also sees each feature's most extreme value. Chosen on validation scenarios only.

  8. The VAE had a hair trigger

    Inputs that are almost always zero in normal traffic let one telnet connection set off an alarm on its own. Each input's influence is now capped, so a window must be unusual in several ways at once.

  9. Was the benchmark detecting its own insertion?

    Added controls: plain normal traffic inserted the same way. They set a noise floor for every result and show the GAT is not keying on the insertion.

  10. X-IIoTID's rounded timestamps made attacks look trivial

    Timing alone caught 95.7 % of attack windows on its own test split. Running it through the benchmark instead showed the real picture: the rule drops to 74.5 %, the VAE stays at 90.7 %.

  11. Two chatty devices caused nearly all X-IIoTID false alarms

    Lowering one network-wide alarm line would have cost detection. Each device now gets its own line from its own normal traffic, never below the network's: false alarms went from 2.6 % to 1.1 % with no attacks lost.

  12. One model for every network was tried and rejected

    Adding X-IIoTID to the shared models made IoT-23 clearly worse (83.6 % → 60.3 %), so it was rolled back.

  13. ACI-IoT-2023 wrote its dates day-first

    "2023-01-11" means 1 November. The reader tries both orders and keeps the date inside the capture week; all 1.23 M rows were placed correctly, run on Kaggle without downloading the data.

  14. Gotham's saved alarm line gave a fake 100 %

    With the line stored in the model, 6 % of plain normal traffic was flagged too. Recalibrating on the network's own normal traffic brought it to 96.5 % caught, 0.19 % false alarms and a 0 % noise floor.

  15. Recalibration can break on a one-sided calibration sample

    On NF-ToN-IoT the normal traffic available for calibration came almost entirely from one device's repetitive connections; the recalibrated alarm lines dropped about 100-fold and flagged everything. That run was discarded and the saved alarm lines kept, so calibration data must cover the network's devices.

Limits

What these numbers do not show yet

  • The benchmark is still built from lab recordings. Most normal test periods last only 4–20 minutes, so "100× slower" attacks often had to run faster than that, and the honeypot's own traffic will be the real test.
  • Edge-IIoTset is easy. Its sensors only send MQTT readings, so almost any attack stands out; its near-100 % reflects the data more than the model.
  • Kitsune's VAE flags any new behaviour. Another device's normal traffic is flagged 92 % of the time, and its Mirai network needs calibration (37 % false alarms without it).
  • The GAT mostly does not generalise to unseen attack types (4–69 % when a type is left out of training; ransomware is the exception at 83 %).
  • Ransomware still has few examples: 971 connections in Edge-IIoTset (7 test windows) and 3,971 in NF-ToN-IoT-v3 (4 injected attacks). Two sources now agree (99.8 % and 93–99 %), but neither number is statistically strong.
  • NF-ToN-IoT-v3 has 3.5 % false alarms, above the 1 % target, a 75 % noise floor for another device's traffic, and only 1.8 hours of normal test traffic. Its slowed-down scans from 24 April are caught 59–64 % of the time.
  • IoT-23 has almost no clean normal traffic. Its false-alarm numbers are unreliable, a one-line rule beats the models there, and it may be replaced by a better dataset.
  • X-IIoTID rests on 12 injected attacks, always placed on the busiest devices, never on the two chatty ones that got higher alarm lines. Its LSTM alone is not useful there, and its ransomware data is unusable.
  • Gotham's devices are emulated, so their normal traffic is very regular and foreign traffic stands out more than on real hardware; its false-alarm figure rests on only 18 minutes of normal test traffic (513 windows). ACI's rests on 2.8 hours, and its floods come from a single attacker, not a botnet.
  • The LSTM's own alarm is weak on varied networks (1.6 % on ACI, 4.8 % on X-IIoTID, 28 % on Gotham): it rebuilds simple, repetitive attacks too well. It is still needed as the stage that summarises each window for the VAE.
  • "Closest known attack" works within a dataset but transfers poorly across them; Kitsune's SSDP flood does not resemble the other datasets' floods.
  • Not yet built: the attack-analysis stage, the RAG-LLM Blue Team report, and live scoring through the GAT. Their inputs are already produced by this pipeline.
